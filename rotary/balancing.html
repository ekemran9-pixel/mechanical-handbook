Balancing

Unbalance types, balance grades, single and two-plane field balancing with trial weights and the vector method.

Types of unbalance

  • Static: heavy spot on one side. The rotor turns to rest heavy side down. Fixed in one plane.
  • Couple: two equal heavy spots 180° apart on opposite ends. Statically balanced but wobbles. Needs two planes.
  • Dynamic: a mix of both. The general case on wide rotors. Needs two planes.

Rule of thumb: rotors with length/diameter below about 0.5 (fans, pulleys, narrow impellers) can often be done in one plane.

Balance quality grades (ISO 21940-11, was ISO 1940)

GradeTypical rotor
G40Car wheels, crankshafts of slow engines
G16Agricultural machinery, crushers
G6.3Fans, pump impellers, flywheels, general machinery
G2.5Turbines, compressors, motor rotors, high speed pumps, machine tool drives
G1Grinders, tape recorders, precision spindles
Permissible residual unbalance U (g·mm) = 9549 × G × M ÷ n

M = rotor mass (kg), n = rpm, G = grade. Divide U by the correction radius (mm) to get grams. Split between planes as the rotor geometry requires (half each for a symmetrical rotor).

Example: 120 kg fan impeller at 1480 rpm, G6.3: U = 9549 × 6.3 × 120 ÷ 1480 = 4,878 g·mm. At 400 mm radius: 12.2 g total.

Before balancing

  • Confirm it's unbalance: dominant 1X radial, phase steady, H/V phase ~90° apart.
  • Clean the rotor. Deposits cause most fan unbalance.
  • Check for looseness, cracked blades, bearing wear, eccentric pulleys, resonance. Balancing can't fix those.
  • Check the rotor isn't rubbing.

Single-plane field balancing (with phase)

  1. Mount the sensor radially on the bearing nearest the plane. Mount the tacho on a reflective mark on the shaft.
  2. Run 0 (original): record 1X amplitude and phase (e.g. 8.0 mm/s at 60°).
  3. Choose a trial weight that changes the reading by at least 30% (or gives a force of about 10% of rotor weight). Fit it at a known angle and radius. Record its mass and position.
  4. Run 1: record new amplitude and phase (e.g. 5.0 mm/s at 140°).
  5. The instrument (or vector math) works out the effect of the trial weight: T = Run1 − Run0.
  6. Correction weight = trial weight × (|O| ÷ |T|), placed at angle shifted by the angle needed to bring T opposite O.
  7. Remove the trial weight (unless told to leave it) and fit the correction.
  8. Run 2: check. Trim if needed.

Angle conventions

Measure angles in the same direction every time. Most instruments say "against rotation" or "with rotation". Mark the rotor with angles before starting. If the phase moved the wrong way after the first correction, check the convention.

Four-run method (no phase)

Used when there is no tacho. Take the original amplitude, then run with the trial weight at 0°, 120° and 240°. Draw circles with radii equal to each reading on a polar plot; their intersection gives the correction. Slower but needs only an amplitude meter.

Two-plane balancing

Two sensors (one per bearing), two trial weights (one per plane, one at a time). Runs: original, trial in plane 1, trial in plane 2. The instrument solves the influence coefficients and gives correction weights for both planes. Keep the same sensor positions throughout.

Fitting weights

  • Weld-on weights: weld only with the welding earth clamped on the rotor, never through bearings. Use a qualified welder and approved procedure.
  • Bolt-on and clip-on weights: check they can't come loose at speed. Use thread locker.
  • Grinding or drilling to remove metal: check with engineering for strength.
  • Record final weights, positions and readings on the job card.
Thrown weights kill. Stay out of the plane of rotation during runs. Guard fitted for every run.
