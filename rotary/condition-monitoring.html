Condition monitoring

Thermography, ultrasound, motor current analysis, performance monitoring and setting up an inspection route.

On this page

  2. Thermography
  3. Airborne and contact ultrasound
  4. Motor current signature analysis (MCSA)

The P-F curve

A fault starts at a point (P) where it can first be detected, and grows to functional failure (F). Different methods see it at different times:

  1. Ultrasound and oil analysis (earliest)
  2. Vibration analysis
  3. Thermography
  4. Audible noise
  5. Hot to touch
  6. Failure

Inspection intervals should be shorter than half the P-F interval, so you catch the fault at least once before it fails.

Thermography

  • Finds hot bearings, misaligned couplings, blocked coolers, refractory failures, steam trap failures, electrical hot joints.
  • Emissivity matters. Shiny metal reads low. Use electrical tape or paint as a reference spot.
  • Compare similar items under similar load (phase A vs B vs C, pump A vs pump B). A ΔT of 10 °C between like items is worth investigating; above 40 °C is serious for electrical joints.
  • Electrical scans need at least 40% load and qualified people with switchgear access.

Airborne and contact ultrasound

  • Hears friction and impacts above human hearing (20–100 kHz, usually 30–40 kHz).
  • Uses: bearing condition and greasing, compressed air and gas leaks, steam traps, valve passing, electrical arcing and corona.
  • Greasing with ultrasound: listen while greasing, stop when the dB reading drops to its lowest and starts to rise again.
  • An increase of 8 dB above baseline: lack of lubrication. 12–16 dB: early failure. 35 dB+: approaching failure.

Motor current signature analysis (MCSA)

Measures current in one phase with a clamp. Shows broken rotor bars (sidebands at pole pass frequency around line frequency), eccentricity, and some load problems. Done from the MCC without going near the motor.

Performance monitoring

  • Pumps: head, flow, power: compare with the curve to see internal wear.
  • Compressors: efficiency, discharge temperature, ratio, valve temperatures on recips.
  • Heat exchangers: approach temperatures, ΔP, fouling factor.
  • Fans: ΔP and amps.

Setting up a route

  1. Rank the equipment by criticality (safety, production, cost of failure, redundancy).
  2. Choose methods per machine (vibration, oil, thermography, ultrasound).
  3. Set intervals: monthly for critical rotating, quarterly for less critical; continuous online monitoring for the most critical turbomachinery.
  4. Mark measurement points. Take baselines after overhaul.
  5. Set alarm levels (absolute and trend-based).
  6. Write a short report for each alarm with a clear action and a due date.
  7. Close the loop: what was found when it was opened, and was the call right?
