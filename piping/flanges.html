Flanges

Flange types

TypeNotes
Weld neck (WN)Butt welded, tapered hub. Best for high pressure, temperature and cyclic loads. Bore must match pipe schedule
Slip-on (SO)Slides over pipe, two fillet welds. Cheap, easy to align. Lower fatigue strength (about 2/3 of WN)
Socket weld (SW)Small bore, single fillet weld
ThreadedSmall bore, no welding (non-hazardous, low pressure)
Lap jointLoose backing flange over a stub end. Rotates for bolt alignment; flange can be carbon steel over a stainless stub end
BlindCloses the end
Orifice flangeWN with pressure tappings and jackscrews, for flow measurement
Spectacle blind / spade and spacerPositive isolation between flanges
Long weld neck, nipo flange, swivel ring, anchor flangeSpecial uses

Standards

  • ASME B16.5: NPS ½ to 24, Classes 150, 300, 400, 600, 900, 1500, 2500.
  • ASME B16.47: NPS 26 to 60. Series A (MSS SP-44, heavier) and Series B (API 605, lighter). They don't bolt together.
  • EN 1092-1: PN 6, 10, 16, 25, 40, 63, 100…
  • API 6A: wellhead flanges (2,000 to 20,000 psi).

Facings

  • RF (raised face): 1.6 mm (Class 150/300) or 6.4 mm (Class 400 and up) raised. Serrated spiral or concentric finish 3.2–6.3 µm Ra (125–250 µin) for spiral wound and sheet gaskets.
  • FF (flat face): full face gasket. Cast iron, FRP, rubber-lined. Never bolt an RF steel flange to FF cast iron: it cracks.
  • RTJ (ring type joint): grooved face for metal ring gaskets. High pressure.
  • Tongue and groove, male and female: special.

Flange face damage limits (ASME PCC-1 Appendix D)

  • Radial scratches across the gasket seating area are the most serious.
  • For spiral wound gaskets: radial defects deeper than the serrations or wider than about 1.5 mm across more than 50% of the seating width need repair (machining).
  • Check with a straight edge and a feeler for flatness (warping).
